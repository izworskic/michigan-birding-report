How Chris Izworski Reads Michigan Bird Migration

A practical way to combine migration radar, recent bird reports, weather, geography, and morning field evidence without asking any one data source to tell you more than it actually knows.

By Chris Izworski · Published August 24, 2026

Migration data works best as a chain of evidence. BirdCast can tell me that birds are moving through the atmosphere. eBird can tell me what people have recently reported on the ground. Weather and geography help explain where migrants may concentrate. The morning field check is where the prediction meets reality.

1. Start with the right question

I do not begin by asking, “What birds will be at this hotspot tomorrow?” No single migration product can answer that reliably. I begin with smaller questions: How much nocturnal migration is expected? What direction is it moving? What species have recently been reported in the region? What weather and landscape features could concentrate those birds after sunrise?

Breaking the problem into those pieces keeps a forecast from turning into false precision.

2. Use BirdCast for movement, not species identification

BirdCast uses weather-surveillance radar and migration models to describe large-scale bird movement. Its forecast maps are designed to estimate migration intensity, and its live products show actual nocturnal movement detected by radar during migration periods.

The important limitation is equally useful: radar does not identify the species creating each movement signal. A high migration night tells me to pay attention. It does not tell me that a particular warbler, shorebird, or thrush will be standing at a particular location at dawn.

Radar answers “how much and which way?” Species identification has to come from other evidence, especially recent observations, seasonal timing, habitat, and field verification.

3. Read the direction as well as the volume

A big number alone is not enough. I look at the direction of movement and the weather pattern around it. Tailwinds can help migrants cover distance; fronts and unfavorable winds can slow movement or change where birds stop. The practical question is whether the atmospheric movement is likely to put birds into the part of Michigan I can actually reach the next morning.

This is where Michigan's shape matters. The Great Lakes are not background scenery. Shorelines, bays, peninsulas, points, river corridors, wetlands, and forest edges can all influence how migrants move and where birders can encounter concentrations.

4. Use eBird to learn what is already on the ground

After the migration signal, I move to eBird. I use recent regional and hotspot reports to understand which species are being observed, where they are being reported, and whether there is a pattern across several checklists rather than one isolated sighting.

eBird hotspots are communal locations: observations from many birders are aggregated around a place. That makes them useful for seeing recent activity, but the list is still evidence of reported birds, not a guarantee of what will be present when I arrive.

County-level summaries help with the wider picture. Hotspots help with local planning. Species maps help answer a different question: where has this species been reported recently?

5. Favor repeated evidence over a single rarity

A rare-bird report can be worth pursuing, but it is not the same thing as a migration pattern. For a general morning plan, I give more weight to several recent checklists showing similar turnover or arrival than to one exceptional record.

I also look at recency. A report from yesterday morning is usually more useful for tomorrow's decision than a high species count from several weeks ago, especially during fast migration windows.

6. Let habitat narrow the prediction

Once I know birds are moving and I have a recent species picture, habitat becomes the filter. Shorebirds need different conditions than woodland migrants. Waterfowl, marsh birds, raptors, and passerines do not distribute themselves randomly across a county.

A Saginaw Bay mudflat, a wooded shoreline edge, a marsh, and a city park can all sit under the same migration radar signal while offering very different birds. The local habitat determines which part of the regional movement is relevant.

7. Saginaw Bay: a practical example

Suppose BirdCast shows a strong migration night over the Great Lakes region. I would first note the volume and direction, then check recent eBird reports around Saginaw Bay and nearby counties. If several checklists show new arrivals or increasing diversity, I look for the habitat that matches the species groups moving through.

For shorebirds, water level and exposed edge matter. For woodland migrants, shelter, vegetation, and the relationship to the shoreline matter. For waterfowl, open water and wind exposure may matter more. The “best spot” therefore changes with the birds and the conditions.

The next morning I verify. If the field does not match the model, the field wins.

8. Timing matters after a migration night

Nocturnal migrants are often most useful to a birder after they have landed. That makes early morning a natural field window, but the exact timing depends on light, weather, habitat, and the species involved. A migration dashboard is not a promise that all of the night's movement will remain locally available through the day.

That is why Michigan Birding Report treats current sightings and migration context as complementary signals rather than substituting one for the other.

9. The hierarchy I use

  1. Movement: BirdCast forecast and live migration intensity.
  2. Direction: where the movement is headed and the weather driving it.
  3. Recent species: eBird region, hotspot, and species reports.
  4. Recency: favor evidence from the current migration window.
  5. Habitat: match expected birds to real places.
  6. Geography: account for Great Lakes shorelines, bays, points, wetlands, and corridors.
  7. Field check: observations on the ground override the forecast.

Primary sources

The framework uses public scientific and community-observation systems as inputs. The interpretation is mine; the underlying data belongs to the organizations that produce it.

  • BirdCast — migration forecasts, live migration maps, and radar-based migration science from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and partners.
  • BirdCast migration tools — forecast and live products for nocturnal migration.
  • eBird Explore — recent species, region, hotspot, and checklist observations.
  • eBird Help Center — documentation for interpreting hotspots, regions, maps, and observations.
  • National Weather Service — official weather forecasts and observations used to understand the conditions around migration.
The operating rule: use models to choose where to look, community observations to refine the target, and the field to decide what is actually there.

The quick read

  • BirdCast = movement.
  • eBird = recent reports.
  • Weather = explanation.
  • Habitat = filter.
  • Field observation = truth.
